Federal crash records, 2016–2024

What is killing people on America’s roads

Every death on a US public road is recorded in the Fatality Analysis Reporting System. We analysed all 353,706 deaths recorded from 2016 to 2024 to find out who died, what was involved, and which risks were involved in the most deaths.

Key findings

These records answer best how much less often belted occupants and helmeted riders died than their unbelted or unhelmeted companions in the same vehicle, which conditions go with a crash being fatal once other circumstances are held fixed, and how the records themselves behave. They cannot give risk per mile, the effect of any enforcement or campaign, or the effect of drugs, and every estimate here describes crashes that were reported or fatal, not causes.

Overview

Most deaths involved drink-driving, speeding, or a victim without a belt or helmet

Police and NHTSA code each fatal crash for speeding, and NHTSA estimates every driver’s blood alcohol concentration, imputing it statistically where no test result exists. For most people killed, the record shows whether they wore a seat belt or helmet. These four behaviours are the best-measured in the records, and all four are long-standing targets of road-safety law and enforcement.

Together they were involved in 60% of deaths: 210,946 people from 2016 to 2024. They often came together. In 25% of deaths two or more were present. Crashes with both a drunk driver and speeding, for example, accounted for 11% of all deaths.

Distraction (8% of deaths) and drowsy driving (2%) are also coded, but police can seldom establish them after a crash, so they are left out of this count. Drug tests are given mainly to drivers who died, and testing practice differs by state, so drug involvement is not counted either.

Sources: deaths/segments, deaths/behaviour_summary, deaths/behaviour_overlap. Observed counts; alcohol estimated from ten imputations.

Drunk driving

About 11,800 people a year on average died in drunk-driving crashes, and between 27% and 39% of them were not the drunk driver

Of the 105,821 people killed from 2016 to 2024 in crashes in which a driver had a blood alcohol concentration of .08 or more, 61% were the drunk drivers themselves. The rest were their passengers (13%), people in other vehicles (15%) and pedestrians or cyclists (11%): about 41,700 people over the nine years. That count includes drivers whose blood alcohol NHTSA imputed rather than measured (41% of the drivers at .08 or more). Counting only measured results, 27% of the dead were not the drunk driver; the measured-only figure is itself selected by who was tested, so the two figures bound the share rather than pin it down.

Who the drunk drivers were

Most drunk drivers in fatal crashes were far over the limit. 66% had a blood alcohol concentration of .15 or more, nearly twice the legal limit. 24% were driving without a valid licence, against 12% of drivers with no alcohol.

Yet only 7.4% had a DWI conviction on their record from the previous five years (9.5% among drivers with a measured blood alcohol result, 4.1% among those whose result was imputed). Driver records miss some convictions and their completeness varies by state, so the true share is higher, but most of these drivers had no recent conviction for measures aimed at convicted drivers, such as court-ordered ignition interlocks, to act on.

NHTSA imputes blood alcohol for drivers who were not tested, from the circumstances of the crash; the figures here average its ten imputations. Driver records show convictions, not arrests, and can miss offences in other states.

Sources: deaths/victim_roles, deaths/victim_roles_bac_basis, deaths/driver_records, deaths/driver_records_measured_bac, deaths/driver_bac_imputed_share. Estimated from ten imputations unless marked measured only.

Seat belts

49% of people killed in passenger vehicles were unbelted, where belt use was recorded

Among people killed in cars, SUVs, pickups and vans whose belt use was recorded, 48.6% were unbelted: 94,400 deaths from 2016 to 2024. Belt use was not recorded for another 10%; counting all of them as belted, or all as unbelted, gives a range of 44% to 54%.

Not wearing a belt was most common at night (56%, against 41% by day), in pickups (60%), among men (53%) and among adults aged 25–34 (60%).

How many lives belts would have saved

Comparing people in the same vehicle in the same crash holds the crash itself constant. In fatal crashes, and as belt use is recorded, belted drivers and front passengers were 66% less likely to die than their unbelted companion. Survivors who were in fact unbelted are sometimes recorded as belted, which inflates that figure. If one in twenty belted survivors was misrecorded, the reduction would be 52%; if one in ten, 38%. Records can also err the other way, coding someone who died unbelted when they were belted. With one in ten belted survivors and one in twenty unbelted-coded deaths misrecorded, the reduction would be 28%. The benefit would disappear if about 21% of belted survivors were misrecorded.

The pooled figure also averages very different kinds of crash. The reduction was 51% in multi-vehicle angle crashes and 84% in single-vehicle crashes that began with a rollover. In an exploratory follow-up, belts were somewhat less protective within the same type of crash when the driver was speeding (ratio of belt risk ratios 1.10 to 1.24 across four ways of grouping crashes, 1.24 (1.13–1.35) when single- and multi-vehicle and rollover crashes are grouped together), and no different when the driver was drinking (1.02 (0.93–1.11)).

Applied to the 94,400 unbelted occupants who died, these estimates mean that up to about 6,900 of them a year on average from 2016 to 2024 would have been expected to survive had they been belted, as recorded and leaving out the 20,998 deaths whose belt use is unknown. 61% of the unbelted deaths were in vehicles with one occupant, where no within-vehicle comparison exists. The scenarios: about 5,500 if one in twenty belted survivors was misrecorded, 4,000 if one in ten and 500 if one in five. The records cannot say how common misrecording is, so the size of the benefit is uncertain.

These figures estimate what belts would have meant for the people who died. They are not a forecast of what any law or campaign would achieve. The effectiveness was measured on drivers and front passengers aged 13 and over and is applied here to every seat and age.

Sources: deaths/restraint, deaths/potential_lives, deaths/potential_lives_context, matched/belt_effect, matched/belt_misreporting_two_sided_dpc, matched/belt_by_crash_type, restraint_behaviour/dpc_contrast_within_configuration. Observed counts; effectiveness from a within-vehicle comparison (double pair comparison).

Speed

Speeding was involved in 28% of deaths, and 37% of those killed were not the speeding driver

From 2016 to 2024, 98,693 people died in crashes in which police recorded a driver as speeding. 63% were the speeding drivers. The others were their passengers (16%), people in other vehicles (15%) and pedestrians or cyclists (6%).

Exceeding the limit was the most often fatal kind of speeding

Police record two kinds of speeding: exceeding the posted limit (or racing), and driving too fast for conditions. In the national sample of police-reported crashes, a crash in which a driver exceeded the limit was 5.2× as likely to be fatal as a comparable crash with no speeding, after adjusting for the road, light, weather, alcohol and the drivers and vehicles involved. Crashes coded “too fast for conditions”, which make up most of the speeding category and happen on wet roads far more often than other crashes (24% of them, against 13% of crashes without speeding), showed a small association with a fatal outcome that the data cannot tell apart from none (risk ratio 1.06 (0.89–1.26)). That interval rules out only associations larger than about 22 percent either way, and when the type of crash is also held fixed the ratio is 1.29 (1.13–1.47). Splitting speeding by type was an exploratory analysis, prompted by earlier results rather than planned in advance, so treat these figures as leads.

Speeding drivers in fatal crashes also had worse records than drivers who were neither speeding nor drinking: 24% had no valid licence, against 10%, and 24% had a speeding conviction in the previous five years, against 16%.

Speeding is a judgement police make after the crash. FARS records it for more fatal crashes than the police-reported sample does, and recording practice differs between states and agencies.

Sources: deaths/victim_roles, deaths/driver_records, severity/fatal_speeding_type, severity/speeding_type_models. Observed counts (FARS); survey estimates with design-based confidence intervals (CRSS).

Pedestrians

Pedestrians die mostly in the dark, between intersections, on major urban roads

60,876 pedestrians were killed from 2016 to 2024, 17% of all deaths, and 13% more died in 2024 than in 2019. 82% died in urban areas. 73% were struck on the roadway away from an intersection, and 20% in daylight. Arterials, the main through roads below interstates and freeways, accounted for 38% (principal) and 23% (minor). In 23% of pedestrian deaths the driver fled.

Pedestrians struck on faster roads were far more likely to die

Among pedestrians struck in police-reported crashes, 2% died where the limit was 25 mph or less, 15% at 40–45 mph and 33% at 50–55 mph. Holding fixed the light, the location, the time of day, the day of the week, the year, the region, urban or rural area, alcohol, whether the driver was speeding, the pedestrian’s age and sex, and the type of striking vehicle, the risk at 50–55 mph was still 6.6× that at 25 mph or less. 30% of pedestrian deaths were on roads limited to 40–45 mph. Without adjusting for location, light and time of day the ratio at 50–55 mph would be 12.2×, so part of the gradient reflects where and when fast roads are used. The posted limit is unknown for 30% of struck pedestrians who survived but only 11% of those who died, which makes the raw shares by limit unreliable.

Being struck by a larger vehicle was also more often fatal. Compared with being struck by a car, the adjusted risk of death was 1.25× as high when the vehicle was an SUV, 1.32× for a pickup and 2.1× for a large truck or bus. SUVs struck 19% of the pedestrians killed in 2016 and 25% in 2024.

The records contain no count of how much people walk, so they show where pedestrians died, not where walking is most dangerous per trip.

Sources: deaths/pedestrians, vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts, vulnerable/fars_pedestrian_deaths_by_striking_vehicle, vulnerable/pedestrian_speed_limit_adjustment, vulnerable/speed_limit_missingness. Observed counts (FARS); survey estimates (CRSS).

Motorcycles

Motorcyclist deaths rose 23% from 2019 to 2024, and 38% of those killed wore no helmet, where recorded

51,254 motorcyclists died from 2016 to 2024, 14% of all deaths. Unlike deaths in cars and light trucks, motorcyclist deaths did not fall after 2021. Among those whose helmet use was recorded, 38% wore none, down from 40% in 2016 to 34% in 2024.

A rider and a passenger on the same motorcycle share the same crash, which makes them a natural comparison. In fatal crashes, helmeted riders and passengers were 31% less likely to die than an unhelmeted companion (95% confidence interval 20% to 40%). Applied to the 18,789 unhelmeted motorcyclists who died, that means about 640 a year on average from 2016 to 2024 would have been expected to survive with a helmet, leaving out the 1,665 deaths whose helmet use is unknown.

Treat that figure with care. If one in twenty helmeted survivors was misrecorded, the reduction would be 4%, and it would vanish at about 6% misrecorded. It rests on 455 rider-passenger pairs that differ in helmet use. Only 16% of the unhelmeted motorcyclists who died were in a rider-passenger pair of any kind (not only pairs that differ in helmet use), and 84% were riding alone, so applying the estimate to all of them assumes riders with a passenger resemble riders without one.

Helmet use is missing for fewer of the dead (3%) than belt use (10%). The records contain no count of how much people ride, so changes in motorcyclist deaths cannot be split into more riding and more risk.

Sources: deaths/helmet, deaths/potential_lives, matched/double_pair_comparison, matched/helmet_misreporting_dpc, matched/helmet_pair_coverage. Observed counts; effectiveness from a rider–passenger comparison.

Vehicles

Even after adjustment, occupants of old vehicles were far more likely to die in a crash

In the national sample of police-reported crashes, occupants of cars, SUVs, pickups and vans 18 or more years old died 4.3× as often as occupants of vehicles under 4 years old. Part of that gap reflects who drives old vehicles, and where. After adjusting for the occupant’s age, sex, seat and belt use, the type of vehicle and of any other vehicle involved, and the time, light, weather, road, area, drivers’ ages, speeding and alcohol, the risk was still 2.2× as high (95% confidence interval 2.0–2.5). Also holding fixed the type of crash and the number of vehicles, which may partly reflect the vehicle itself, it was 1.8× (1.6–1.9).

Old vehicles also carried a growing share of the dead. Vehicles 18 or more years old held 18% of occupants killed in 2016 and 30% in 2024. Part of that rise reflects an ageing fleet: the records do not show how many old vehicles are on the road.

Large trucks

13% of deaths, 47,175 people, were in crashes involving a large truck. 85% of those killed were not the truck driver, and 71% were in another vehicle. That describes who was in these crashes. It does not show who was at fault.

Sources: person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted, deaths/vehicle_age, deaths/victim_roles. Survey estimates (CRSS); observed counts (FARS).

Since 2019

Deaths rose among the fastest in risky-driving crashes and on urban roads, and deaths on foot, on bicycles and on motorcycles have stayed high

Deaths rose from 36,355 in 2019 to 43,230 in 2021, then fell to 39,254 in 2024, still 8% above 2019. Deaths rose among the fastest in crashes involving risky driving: from 2019 to 2021, deaths in crashes with a driver who had no valid licence rose 44%, hit-and-run deaths 43%, drunk-driving deaths 33% and speeding deaths 30%.

By 2024, deaths of people in cars, SUVs, pickups and vans were back near their 2019 level (+2%). The rise was urban: deaths on urban roads were +15% against 2019, and on rural roads −2%. Deaths of people on foot, on bicycles and on motorcycles stayed high: motorcyclists +23%, cyclists +28% and pedestrians +13%.

The drivers in fatal crashes changed too. The share of drivers in fatal crashes without a valid licence rose from 13.0% in 2019 to 16.7% in 2020, and was 13.9% in 2024.

The records contain no miles driven. Travel fell in 2020, so these counts do not show how much riskier each mile became.

Sources: deaths/annual, deaths/segments, deaths/driver_records_by_year. Observed counts; alcohol estimated from ten imputations.

Limits

What these records cannot tell you

Risk per mile or per trip
The records count deaths and crashes. They contain no miles driven, walked or ridden, and no population, so they show where deaths concentrate, not how dangerous each activity is.
Exact behaviour
Speeding, belt use and alcohol are what police and NHTSA recorded after the crash. Killed drivers are tested far more often than survivors, but police-recorded alcohol is more often unknown in fatal crashes, and survivors’ belt and helmet use is largely what they told police. Blood alcohol is imputed for drivers who were not tested.
Drugs
Drug tests are given mainly to drivers who died, and testing practice differs by state, so drug involvement cannot be compared and is not counted here.
Cause
A behaviour present in a fatal crash did not necessarily cause it. The estimates of lives belts and helmets would have saved are model-based and depend on how accurately belt and helmet use were recorded.
What policies achieved
The records contain no measure of enforcement, cameras, testing or campaigns, so they cannot show what any policy achieved.

Method

Data and method

The analysis uses only two NHTSA data systems, 2016–2024, as published. FARS is a census of every crash on a US public road in which someone died within 30 days. CRSS is a national probability sample of police-reported crashes of every severity, the only source of how often a crash of a given kind is fatal. Rows from the two systems are never combined.

Counts of deaths are observed FARS counts using NHTSA’s own analytical definitions of speeding-related crashes, belt and helmet use and person type. Drunk-driving crashes are those in which a driver’s blood alcohol concentration was .08 or more in NHTSA’s multiple imputation; every alcohol figure averages its ten imputations. CRSS estimates use the survey weights, strata and primary sampling units, with 95% confidence intervals. Adjusted risk ratios come from survey-weighted logistic models with standardised margins. Belt and helmet effectiveness come from comparing people in the same vehicle (double pair comparison).
