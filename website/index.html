US road deaths, 2016–2024

39,254 people died on US roads in 2024. This site uses the federal crash records for 2016–2024 to show who died, how that changed, and in which circumstances crashes were most often fatal.

Sources: NHTSA FARS (every fatal crash) and CRSS (a national sample of police-reported crashes). Independent analysis; not affiliated with NHTSA.

1 · DEATHS BY YEAR

Deaths rose sharply in 2020–21 and are still above 2019

Road deaths were 36,355 in 2019. They rose to 39,007 in 2020 and 43,230 in 2021, then fell for three years to 39,254 in 2024 — still 8% more than in 2019.

These are counts. The records contain no miles driven, so they do not show whether travel became more dangerous per mile.

2 · WHO DIED

Most of the increase since 2019 was among motorcyclists and pedestrians

Between 2019 and 2024, motorcyclist deaths rose 23% (5,044 to 6,227) and pedestrian deaths 13% (6,272 to 7,080). Deaths of people inside cars, SUVs, pickups and vans rose 2% (22,370 to 22,739). Motorcyclists and pedestrians were 31% of deaths in 2019 but account for 69% of the increase.

2016–2024: pedestrian deaths +16%, cyclist deaths +29%, passenger deaths −12%.

Changes in how much people walked, cycled or rode are not recorded, so these changes cannot be split into more travel and more risk.

3 · PEDESTRIANS

A growing share of pedestrians are killed by SUVs

SUVs struck 1,149 of the pedestrians killed in 2016 and 1,746 in 2024, a rise from 19% to 25% of pedestrian deaths. The share killed by passenger cars fell from 42% to 36%.

Speed and darkness

In police-reported crashes, the share of struck pedestrians who died rises steeply with the road's speed limit: 2.2% where the limit is 25 mph or less, 14.8% at 40–45 mph and 33.1% at 50–55 mph. At night on unlit roads 25.9% died, against 2.6% in daylight.

Vehicle type at the same speed limit

On roads limited to 35 mph or less, 7.0% of pedestrians struck by an SUV or pickup died, against 4.3% of those struck by a car — 1.6 times as often, after adjusting for pedestrian age and sex, crash location, light, time of day, area, year, and recorded alcohol and speeding.

Vehicle type is unknown for about 11% of pedestrian deaths. The data have no count of vehicles on the road by type, so a rising SUV share partly reflects more SUVs. Impact speed is not recorded; the posted limit stands in for it.

4 · TIME OF DAY

Crashes between midnight and 6 a.m. are four times as likely to be fatal as crashes from 6 to 10 a.m.

Of police-reported crashes between midnight and 5:59 a.m., 1.5% were fatal, against 0.38% between 6:00 and 9:59 a.m. These hours have 8% of crashes but 20% of fatal ones.

Rates are per police-reported crash, and minor crashes are less often reported at night. Alcohol and darkness both concentrate in these hours; this chart does not separate them.

CRSS survey estimates; FARS count for the share of fatal crashes · descriptive/frequency_vs_severity.csv

5 · AGE

Occupants aged 75 and over are the most likely to die in a crash

In police-reported crashes, 0.49% of car, SUV, pickup and van occupants aged 75 or older were killed, against 0.18% of those aged 25–34. After adjusting for sex, seat position, belt use, vehicle type and vehicle age, speeding, alcohol, and the recorded road, weather, light and time-of-day conditions, the gap widens to 5.5 times (95% CI 4.7–6.5). Crash type is not adjusted for.

Older bodies tolerate impact forces less well; the data cannot separate frailty from driving and travel patterns.

6 · RURAL ROADS

Two in three people killed on rural roads die at the scene

Of the 151,940 people killed on rural roads in 2016–2024, 67% died at the scene, against 49% of the 200,359 killed on urban roads. In 2024 the median time from the crash to the arrival of emergency medical services, where recorded, was 14 minutes on rural roads and 8 minutes on urban ones.

In 2024 arrival time is recorded for 45% of fatal crashes on rural roads and 38% on urban roads, and only fatal crashes are in FARS, so the data cannot show how many deaths faster care would prevent.

WHAT THESE RECORDS CANNOT SHOW

Limits of the data

  • No miles driven, walked or ridden. The records count deaths and crashes, not risk per trip or per mile.
  • Police reports. Speeding, alcohol, drugs and belt use are what police recorded, and they record more carefully after severe crashes.
  • Drugs. Drug tests are given mostly to drivers who died, so drug involvement cannot be compared across crashes.
  • Associations, not effects. A circumstance that is common in fatal crashes did not necessarily cause them, and the records measure no enforcement or programme.
