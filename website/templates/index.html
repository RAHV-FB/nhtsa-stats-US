What is killing people on America’s roads

Every death on a US public road is recorded in the Fatality Analysis Reporting System. We analysed all {{deaths/segments|segment=all|deaths|,.0f}} deaths recorded from 2016 to 2024 to find out who died, what was involved, and which risks were involved in the most deaths.

Overview

Most deaths involved drink-driving, speeding, or a victim without a belt or helmet

Police and NHTSA code each fatal crash for speeding, and NHTSA estimates every driver’s blood alcohol concentration, imputing it statistically where no test result exists. For most people killed, the record shows whether they wore a seat belt or helmet. These four behaviours are the best-measured in the records, and all four are long-standing targets of road-safety law and enforcement.

Together they were involved in {{deaths/segments|segment=any_of_four|share_of_all|pct:0}} of deaths: {{deaths/segments|segment=any_of_four|deaths|,.0f}} people from 2016 to 2024. They often came together. In {{deaths/segments|segment=two_or_more|share_of_all|pct:0}} of deaths two or more were present. Crashes with both a drunk driver and speeding, for example, accounted for {{deaths/segments|segment=alcohol_and_speeding|share_of_all|pct:0}} of all deaths.

Distraction ({{deaths/segments|segment=distracted|share_of_all|pct:0}} of deaths) and drowsy driving ({{deaths/segments|segment=drowsy|share_of_all|pct:0}}) are also coded, but police can seldom establish them after a crash, so they are left out of this count. Drug tests are given mainly to drivers who died, and testing practice differs by state, so drug involvement is not counted either.

Sources: deaths/segments, deaths/behaviour_summary, deaths/behaviour_overlap. Observed counts; alcohol estimated from ten imputations.

Drunk driving

About {{deaths/segments|segment=alcohol_impaired|per_year|rnd:-2}} people a year died in drunk-driving crashes. {{deaths/victim_roles|group=alcohol_impaired;role=not_risk_driver|share_of_group|pct:0}} of them were not the drunk driver

Of the {{deaths/segments|segment=alcohol_impaired|deaths|,.0f}} people killed from 2016 to 2024 in crashes in which a driver had a blood alcohol concentration of .08 or more, {{deaths/victim_roles|group=alcohol_impaired;role=risk_driver|share_of_group|pct:0}} were the drunk drivers themselves. The rest were their passengers ({{deaths/victim_roles|group=alcohol_impaired;role=risk_passenger|share_of_group|pct:0}}), people in other vehicles ({{deaths/victim_roles|group=alcohol_impaired;role=other_vehicle_occupant|share_of_group|pct:0}}) and pedestrians or cyclists ({{deaths/victim_roles|group=alcohol_impaired;role=pedestrian_or_cyclist|share_of_group|pct:0}}): about {{deaths/victim_roles|group=alcohol_impaired;role=not_risk_driver|deaths|rnd:-2}} people over the nine years.

Who the drunk drivers were

Most drunk drivers in fatal crashes were far over the limit. {{deaths/driver_records|group=bac_08_plus;indicator=bac_15_plus|share|pct:0}} had a blood alcohol concentration of .15 or more, nearly twice the legal limit. {{deaths/driver_records|group=bac_08_plus;indicator=licence_invalid|share|pct:0}} were driving without a valid licence, against {{deaths/driver_records|group=bac_zero;indicator=licence_invalid|share|pct:0}} of drivers with no alcohol.

Yet only {{deaths/driver_records|group=bac_08_plus;indicator=prior_dwi|share|pct:1}} had a DWI conviction on their record from the previous five years. Driver records miss some convictions, so the true share is higher, but most of these drivers had no recent conviction for measures aimed at convicted drivers, such as court-ordered ignition interlocks, to act on.

NHTSA imputes blood alcohol for drivers who were not tested, from the circumstances of the crash; the figures here average its ten imputations. Driver records show convictions, not arrests, and can miss offences in other states.

Sources: deaths/victim_roles, deaths/driver_records. Estimated from ten imputations.

Seat belts

{{deaths/restraint|dimension=overall;level=all|unbelted_share|pct:0}} of people killed in passenger vehicles were unbelted, where belt use was recorded

Among people killed in cars, SUVs, pickups and vans whose belt use was recorded, {{deaths/restraint|dimension=overall;level=all|unbelted_share|pct:1}} were unbelted: {{deaths/restraint|dimension=overall;level=all|unbelted|,.0f}} deaths from 2016 to 2024. Belt use was not recorded for another {{deaths/restraint|dimension=overall;level=all|unknown_share|pct:0}}; counting all of them as belted, or all as unbelted, gives a range of {{deaths/restraint|dimension=overall;level=all|share_if_unknown_protected|pct:0}} to {{deaths/restraint|dimension=overall;level=all|share_if_unknown_unprotected|pct:0}}.

Not wearing a belt was most common at night ({{deaths/restraint|dimension=time_of_day;level=night|unbelted_share|pct:0}}, against {{deaths/restraint|dimension=time_of_day;level=day|unbelted_share|pct:0}} by day), in pickups ({{deaths/restraint|dimension=vehicle;level=pickup|unbelted_share|pct:0}}), among men ({{deaths/restraint|dimension=sex;level=male|unbelted_share|pct:0}}) and among adults aged 25–34 ({{deaths/restraint|dimension=age_band;level=25-34|unbelted_share|pct:0}}).

How many lives belts would have saved

Comparing people in the same vehicle in the same crash holds the crash itself constant. In fatal crashes, and as belt use is recorded, belted drivers and front passengers were {{matched/belt_effect|estimand=fatality_risk_reduction_belt_given_fatal_crash|estimate|pct:0}} less likely to die than their unbelted companion. Survivors who were in fact unbelted are sometimes recorded as belted, which inflates that figure. If one in twenty belted survivors was misrecorded, the reduction would be {{matched/belt_effect|estimand=fatality_risk_reduction_belt_given_fatal_crash_misreport_f05|estimate|pct:0}}; if one in ten, {{matched/belt_effect|estimand=fatality_risk_reduction_belt_given_fatal_crash_misreport_f10|estimate|pct:0}}.

Applied to the {{deaths/potential_lives|measure=seat_belts;scenario=as_recorded|deaths_without|,.0f}} unbelted occupants who died, these estimates mean that up to about {{deaths/potential_lives|measure=seat_belts;scenario=as_recorded|lives_per_year|rnd:-2}} of them a year would have been expected to survive had they been belted: about {{deaths/potential_lives|measure=seat_belts;scenario=misreport_5pct|lives_per_year|rnd:-2}} if one in twenty belted survivors was misrecorded, {{deaths/potential_lives|measure=seat_belts;scenario=misreport_10pct|lives_per_year|rnd:-2}} if one in ten and {{deaths/potential_lives|measure=seat_belts;scenario=misreport_20pct|lives_per_year|rnd:-2}} if one in five. The records cannot say how common misrecording is, so the size of the benefit is uncertain.

These figures estimate what belts would have meant for the people who died. They are not a forecast of what any law or campaign would achieve. The effectiveness was measured on drivers and front passengers aged 13 and over and is applied here to every seat and age.

Sources: deaths/restraint, deaths/potential_lives, matched/belt_effect. Observed counts; effectiveness from a within-vehicle comparison (double pair comparison).

Speed

Speeding was involved in {{deaths/segments|segment=speeding|share_of_all|pct:0}} of deaths, and {{deaths/victim_roles|group=speeding;role=not_risk_driver|share_of_group|pct:0}} of those killed were not the speeding driver

From 2016 to 2024, {{deaths/segments|segment=speeding|deaths|,.0f}} people died in crashes in which police recorded a driver as speeding. {{deaths/victim_roles|group=speeding;role=risk_driver|share_of_group|pct:0}} were the speeding drivers. The others were their passengers ({{deaths/victim_roles|group=speeding;role=risk_passenger|share_of_group|pct:0}}), people in other vehicles ({{deaths/victim_roles|group=speeding;role=other_vehicle_occupant|share_of_group|pct:0}}) and pedestrians or cyclists ({{deaths/victim_roles|group=speeding;role=pedestrian_or_cyclist|share_of_group|pct:0}}).

Exceeding the limit was the most often fatal kind of speeding

Police record two kinds of speeding: exceeding the posted limit (or racing), and driving too fast for conditions. In the national sample of police-reported crashes, a crash in which a driver exceeded the limit was {{severity/fatal_speeding_type|outcome=fatal;factor=speeding_type;level=exceeded_limit_or_racing|adj_rr|x:1}} as likely to be fatal as a comparable crash with no speeding, after adjusting for the road, light, weather, alcohol and the drivers and vehicles involved. Crashes coded “too fast for conditions”, which make up most of the speeding category and happen on wet roads far more often than other crashes ({{severity/fatal_speeding_type|outcome=fatal;factor=speeding_type;level=too_fast_for_conditions|share_on_wet|pct:0}} of them, against {{severity/fatal_speeding_type|outcome=fatal;factor=speeding_type;level=no|share_on_wet|pct:0}} of crashes without speeding), showed no detectable increase in the risk of being fatal (risk ratio {{severity/fatal_speeding_type|outcome=fatal;factor=speeding_type;level=too_fast_for_conditions|adj_rr|ci:2}}).

Speeding drivers in fatal crashes also had worse records than drivers who were neither speeding nor drinking: {{deaths/driver_records|group=speeding;indicator=licence_invalid|share|pct:0}} had no valid licence, against {{deaths/driver_records|group=neither;indicator=licence_invalid|share|pct:0}}, and {{deaths/driver_records|group=speeding;indicator=prior_speeding|share|pct:0}} had a speeding conviction in the previous five years, against {{deaths/driver_records|group=neither;indicator=prior_speeding|share|pct:0}}.

Speeding is a judgement police make after the crash. It is recorded more completely when someone died, and recording practice differs between states and agencies.

Sources: deaths/victim_roles, deaths/driver_records, severity/fatal_speeding_type. Observed counts (FARS); survey estimates with design-based confidence intervals (CRSS).

Pedestrians

Pedestrians die mostly in the dark, between intersections, on major urban roads

{{deaths/segments|segment=pedestrians|deaths|,.0f}} pedestrians were killed from 2016 to 2024, {{deaths/segments|segment=pedestrians|share_of_all|pct:0}} of all deaths, and {{deaths/segments|segment=pedestrians|pct_change_2019_2024|pct:0}} more died in 2024 than in 2019. {{deaths/pedestrians|dimension=area;level=urban|share|pct:0}} died in urban areas. {{deaths/pedestrians|dimension=location;level=midblock_roadway|share|pct:0}} were struck on the roadway away from an intersection, and {{deaths/pedestrians|dimension=light;level=daylight|share|pct:0}} in daylight. Arterials, the main through roads below interstates and freeways, accounted for {{deaths/pedestrians|dimension=road_class;level=principal_arterial|share|pct:0}} (principal) and {{deaths/pedestrians|dimension=road_class;level=minor_arterial|share|pct:0}} (minor). In {{deaths/pedestrians|dimension=hit_and_run;level=yes|share|pct:0}} of pedestrian deaths the driver fled.

Pedestrians struck on faster roads were far more likely to die

Among pedestrians struck in police-reported crashes, {{vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts|victim=pedestrian;outcome=killed;factor=str_speed_limit;level=<=25|crude_rate|pct:0}} died where the limit was 25 mph or less, {{vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts|victim=pedestrian;outcome=killed;factor=str_speed_limit;level=40-45|crude_rate|pct:0}} at 40–45 mph and {{vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts|victim=pedestrian;outcome=killed;factor=str_speed_limit;level=50-55|crude_rate|pct:0}} at 50–55 mph. Holding fixed the light, the location, the time of day, the day of the week, the year, the region, urban or rural area, alcohol, whether the driver was speeding, the pedestrian’s age and sex, and the type of striking vehicle, the risk at 50–55 mph was still {{vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts|victim=pedestrian;outcome=killed;factor=str_speed_limit;level=50-55|adj_rr|x:1}} that at 25 mph or less. {{deaths/pedestrians|dimension=speed_limit;level=40-45|share|pct:0}} of pedestrian deaths were on roads limited to 40–45 mph.

Being struck by a larger vehicle was also more often fatal. Compared with being struck by a car, the adjusted risk of death was {{vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts|victim=pedestrian;outcome=killed;factor=striking;level=suv|adj_rr|x:2}} as high when the vehicle was an SUV, {{vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts|victim=pedestrian;outcome=killed;factor=striking;level=pickup|adj_rr|x:2}} for a pickup and {{vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts|victim=pedestrian;outcome=killed;factor=striking;level=large|adj_rr|x:1}} for a large truck or bus. SUVs struck {{vulnerable/fars_pedestrian_deaths_by_striking_vehicle|YEAR=2016|share_suv|pct:0}} of the pedestrians killed in 2016 and {{vulnerable/fars_pedestrian_deaths_by_striking_vehicle|YEAR=2024|share_suv|pct:0}} in 2024.

The records contain no count of how much people walk, so they show where pedestrians died, not where walking is most dangerous per trip.

Sources: deaths/pedestrians, vulnerable/pedestrian_killed_contrasts, vulnerable/fars_pedestrian_deaths_by_striking_vehicle. Observed counts (FARS); survey estimates (CRSS).

Motorcycles

Motorcyclist deaths rose {{deaths/segments|segment=motorcyclists|pct_change_2019_2024|pct:0}} from 2019 to 2024, and {{deaths/helmet|dimension=overall;level=all|unhelmeted_share|pct:0}} of those killed wore no helmet, where recorded

{{deaths/segments|segment=motorcyclists|deaths|,.0f}} motorcyclists died from 2016 to 2024, {{deaths/segments|segment=motorcyclists|share_of_all|pct:0}} of all deaths. Unlike deaths in cars and light trucks, motorcyclist deaths did not fall after 2021. Among those whose helmet use was recorded, {{deaths/helmet|dimension=overall;level=all|unhelmeted_share|pct:0}} wore none, down from {{deaths/helmet|dimension=year;level=2016|unhelmeted_share|pct:0}} in 2016 to {{deaths/helmet|dimension=year;level=2024|unhelmeted_share|pct:0}} in 2024.

A rider and a passenger on the same motorcycle share the same crash, which makes them a natural comparison. In fatal crashes, helmeted riders and passengers were {{matched/double_pair_comparison|analysis=motorcycle_helmet;misreport_fraction=0;component=combined (inverse-variance weighted)|reduction_1_minus_rr|pct:0}} less likely to die than an unhelmeted companion (95% confidence interval {{matched/double_pair_comparison|analysis=motorcycle_helmet;misreport_fraction=0;component=combined (inverse-variance weighted)|reduction_low|pct:0}} to {{matched/double_pair_comparison|analysis=motorcycle_helmet;misreport_fraction=0;component=combined (inverse-variance weighted)|reduction_high|pct:0}}). Applied to the {{deaths/potential_lives|measure=helmets;scenario=as_recorded|deaths_without|,.0f}} unhelmeted motorcyclists who died, that means about {{deaths/potential_lives|measure=helmets;scenario=as_recorded|lives_per_year|rnd:-1}} a year would have been expected to survive with a helmet.

Helmet use is missing for fewer of the dead ({{deaths/helmet|dimension=overall;level=all|unknown_share|pct:0}}) than belt use ({{deaths/restraint|dimension=overall;level=all|unknown_share|pct:0}}). The records contain no count of how much people ride, so changes in motorcyclist deaths cannot be split into more riding and more risk.

Sources: deaths/helmet, deaths/potential_lives, matched/double_pair_comparison. Observed counts; effectiveness from a rider–passenger comparison.

Vehicles

Even after adjustment, occupants of old vehicles were far more likely to die in a crash

In the national sample of police-reported crashes, occupants of cars, SUVs, pickups and vans 18 or more years old died {{person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted|population=lpv;outcome=killed;factor=veh_age_band;level=18+|crude_rr|x:1}} as often as occupants of vehicles under 4 years old. Part of that gap reflects who drives old vehicles, and where. After adjusting for the occupant’s age, sex, seat and belt use, the type of vehicle and of any other vehicle involved, and the time, light, weather, road, area, drivers’ ages, speeding and alcohol, the risk was still {{person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted|population=lpv;outcome=killed;factor=veh_age_band;level=18+|A_adj_rr|x:1}} as high (95% confidence interval {{person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted|population=lpv;outcome=killed;factor=veh_age_band;level=18+|A_adj_rr_low|.1f}}–{{person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted|population=lpv;outcome=killed;factor=veh_age_band;level=18+|A_adj_rr_high|.1f}}). Also holding fixed the type of crash and the number of vehicles, which may partly reflect the vehicle itself, it was {{person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted|population=lpv;outcome=killed;factor=veh_age_band;level=18+|B_adj_rr|x:1}} ({{person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted|population=lpv;outcome=killed;factor=veh_age_band;level=18+|B_adj_rr_low|.1f}}–{{person/lpv_killed_crude_vs_adjusted|population=lpv;outcome=killed;factor=veh_age_band;level=18+|B_adj_rr_high|.1f}}).

Old vehicles also carried a growing share of the dead. Vehicles 18 or more years old held {{deaths/vehicle_age|YEAR=2016;band=18+|share|pct:0}} of occupants killed in 2016 and {{deaths/vehicle_age|YEAR=2024;band=18+|share|pct:0}} in 2024. Part of that rise reflects an ageing fleet: the records do not show how many old vehicles are on the road.

Since 2019

Deaths rose fastest in risky-driving crashes and on urban roads, and deaths on foot, on bicycles and on motorcycles have stayed high

Deaths rose from {{deaths/segments|segment=all|deaths_2019|,.0f}} in 2019 to {{deaths/segments|segment=all|deaths_2021|,.0f}} in 2021, then fell to {{deaths/segments|segment=all|deaths_2024|,.0f}} in 2024, still {{deaths/segments|segment=all|pct_change_2019_2024|pct:0}} above 2019. Deaths rose fastest in crashes involving risky driving: from 2019 to 2021, deaths in crashes with a driver who had no valid licence rose {{deaths/segments|segment=invalid_licence_crash|pct_change_2019_2021|pct:0}}, hit-and-run deaths {{deaths/segments|segment=hit_and_run|pct_change_2019_2021|pct:0}}, drunk-driving deaths {{deaths/segments|segment=alcohol_impaired|pct_change_2019_2021|pct:0}} and speeding deaths {{deaths/segments|segment=speeding|pct_change_2019_2021|pct:0}}.

By 2024, deaths of people in cars, SUVs, pickups and vans were back near their 2019 level ({{deaths/segments|segment=pv_occupants|pct_change_2019_2024|pctsign:0}}). The rise was urban: deaths on urban roads were {{deaths/segments|segment=urban|pct_change_2019_2024|pctsign:0}} against 2019, and on rural roads {{deaths/segments|segment=rural|pct_change_2019_2024|pctsign:0}}. Deaths of people on foot, on bicycles and on motorcycles stayed high: motorcyclists {{deaths/segments|segment=motorcyclists|pct_change_2019_2024|pctsign:0}}, cyclists {{deaths/segments|segment=cyclists|pct_change_2019_2024|pctsign:0}} and pedestrians {{deaths/segments|segment=pedestrians|pct_change_2019_2024|pctsign:0}}.

The drivers in fatal crashes changed too. The share of drivers in fatal crashes without a valid licence rose from {{deaths/driver_records_by_year|YEAR=2019;group=all;indicator=licence_invalid|share|pct:1}} in 2019 to {{deaths/driver_records_by_year|YEAR=2020;group=all;indicator=licence_invalid|share|pct:1}} in 2020, and was {{deaths/driver_records_by_year|YEAR=2024;group=all;indicator=licence_invalid|share|pct:1}} in 2024.

The records contain no miles driven. Travel fell in 2020, so these counts do not show how much riskier each mile became.

Sources: deaths/annual, deaths/segments, deaths/driver_records_by_year. Observed counts; alcohol estimated from ten imputations.

Limits

What these records cannot tell you

Risk per mile or per trip
The records count deaths and crashes. They contain no miles driven, walked or ridden, and no population, so they show where deaths concentrate, not how dangerous each activity is.
Exact behaviour
Speeding, belt use and alcohol are what police and NHTSA recorded after the crash, and recording is more thorough when someone died. Blood alcohol is imputed for drivers who were not tested.
Drugs
Drug tests are given mainly to drivers who died, and testing practice differs by state, so drug involvement cannot be compared and is not counted here.
Cause
A behaviour present in a fatal crash did not necessarily cause it. The estimates of lives belts and helmets would have saved are model-based.
What policies achieved
The records contain no measure of enforcement, cameras, testing or campaigns, so they cannot show what any policy achieved.

Method

Data and method

The analysis uses only two NHTSA data systems, 2016–2024, as published. FARS is a census of every crash on a US public road in which someone died within 30 days. CRSS is a national probability sample of police-reported crashes of every severity, the only source of how often a crash of a given kind is fatal. Rows from the two systems are never combined.

Counts of deaths are observed FARS counts using NHTSA’s own analytical definitions of speeding-related crashes, belt and helmet use and person type. Drunk-driving crashes are those in which a driver’s blood alcohol concentration was .08 or more in NHTSA’s multiple imputation; every alcohol figure averages its ten imputations. CRSS estimates use the survey weights, strata and primary sampling units, with 95% confidence intervals. Adjusted risk ratios come from survey-weighted logistic models with standardised margins. Belt and helmet effectiveness come from comparing people in the same vehicle (double pair comparison).
